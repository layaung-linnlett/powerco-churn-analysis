# PowerCo Customer Churn Analysis

Predicting churn among small and medium enterprise (SME) electricity customers, and evaluating
whether a proposed 20% retention discount is commercially viable.

Completed as part of the **BCG X Data Science job simulation** on Forage. The client, PowerCo, is
fictional; the datasets are supplied by the programme. All analysis, feature engineering, modelling
and conclusions in this repository are my own work.

---

## Problem

PowerCo's SME division was losing roughly one customer in ten every three months. Management
believed the cause was **price sensitivity** and proposed a blanket 20% discount to stem the losses.

The brief was to test that hypothesis and advise whether the discount should go ahead.

## Result

**The hypothesis was not supported, and the proposed discount would destroy value.**

- Customers who churned and customers who stayed paid **near-identical average prices**
  (€0.1419 vs €0.1424 per kWh). Price *level* does not separate the two groups.
- A blanket 20% discount would cost **€553k** to protect **€324k** of at-risk margin — a loss of
  roughly **€229k**, even under the generous assumption that it retains *every* churner.
- A model-targeted alternative is viable but modest: targeting the highest-risk customers
  (roughly 640 to 660, about 4.5% of the base, depending on the split) returns a mean of **~€33k per year** across repeated splits, against a book worth
  €2.76m. Recommended as a six-month pilot rather than a rollout.

The one-slide summary prepared for the client is in [`reports/executive_summary.pdf`](reports/executive_summary.pdf).

## Data

| File | Rows | Grain |
|---|---|---|
| `client_data.csv` | 14,606 | one row per customer |
| `price_data.csv` | 193,002 | one row per customer per month (2015) |

The two tables join on `id`. Because the price file is a monthly panel, it must be aggregated to
customer level before joining — otherwise every customer record is duplicated twelve times. This
constraint shapes the whole feature engineering stage.

## 1. Exploratory analysis

[`notebooks/01_exploratory_data_analysis.ipynb`](notebooks/01_exploratory_data_analysis.ipynb)

Findings that changed the downstream work:

- **No null values anywhere, but the data is not complete.** 25.5% of `channel_sales` is the literal
  string `"MISSING"`. Those customers churn at 7.6% against a 9.7% baseline, so the missingness is
  itself informative and was retained as its own category rather than imputed.
- **Structural zeros.** `cons_gas_12m` is zero for 82% of customers because they have no gas
  contract; `price_peak_var` is zero for 45% of rows because those customers are on single-period
  tariffs. These are valid values, not missing measurements, and treating them as prices would tell
  a model that half the base has the cheapest electricity available.
- **Severe right skew.** Consumption and margin columns have skew between 4 and 37; `cons_12m` has a
  median of 14,116 kWh against a maximum of 6.2 million. Medians are reported throughout and the
  affected columns are log-transformed before modelling.
- **A duplicated column.** `margin_gross_pow_ele` and `margin_net_pow_ele` are identical in 99.99%
  of rows (correlation 0.9999).
- **Consumption does not separate churners from non-churners.** Plotted on a log scale with each
  group normalised to its own density, the two distributions almost coincide. This negative result
  redirected the analysis away from customer size and towards price movement.

## 2. Feature engineering

[`notebooks/02_feature_engineering.ipynb`](notebooks/02_feature_engineering.ipynb)

Because average price levels did not separate the two groups, the price features were built around
price *movement* rather than price *level*: mean and maximum differences between the off-peak, peak
and mid-peak periods, within-year volatility, and the December-versus-January change.

Contract dates were converted to whole months relative to the January 2016 snapshot. Categories with
fewer than 50 customers were grouped into an "other" level before one-hot encoding — at that size a
0% churn rate is within what chance produces for an average group.

72 columns were generated and then pruned to 51 — 49 model features plus `id` and the target — by
removing pairs correlating above 0.95 and keeping whichever member correlated more strongly with
churn. The pruning surfaced six pairs correlating
at exactly 1.00 among the programme-supplied variance columns, caused by combining variable prices
(~€0.14/kWh) with fixed prices (~€43) on a single scale.

## 3. Modelling

[`notebooks/03_modelling.ipynb`](notebooks/03_modelling.ipynb)

A random forest (1,000 trees) on a 75/25 random train/test split (`random_state=42`), trained on
the **programme-supplied feature file (61 features)**. The feature set built in section 2 (49
features) is not used for this model; it is compared against the supplied one in section 4.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | 0.903 |
| Precision | 0.810 |
| Recall | 0.046 |
| ROC-AUC | 0.665 |

**Accuracy is not a meaningful measure here.** Predicting that nobody churns scores 0.900 on the same
test set, so the model's apparent 0.903 represents an improvement of 0.4 percentage points. With a
90/10 class split, recall and precision are the informative metrics.

**Recall at the default 0.50 threshold is 4.6%** — the model identifies 17 of 366 churners in the
test set. It is highly conservative: when it does flag a customer it is usually right (precision
0.81), but it rarely flags anyone.

**The decision threshold matters more than the model.** Attaching a cost to each outcome — €112 of
margin retained per churner saved, €22 per discount issued — gives a break-even precision of 19.6%.
Sweeping the threshold against that cost model:

| Threshold | Flagged | Churners caught | Recall | Precision | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 (default) | 21 | 17 | 4.6% | 81% | €1,442 |
| 0.30 | 160 | 69 | 18.9% | 43% | **€4,208** |
| 0.15 | 875 | 169 | 46.2% | 19.3% | −€322 |

Moving the cut-off from 0.50 to 0.30 raises recall fourfold and roughly triples the value, with no
change to the model itself. Below 0.15 precision falls under the 19.6% break-even and the programme
starts losing money, as the arithmetic predicts.

## 4. Feature set comparison

[`notebooks/04_feature_set_comparison.ipynb`](notebooks/04_feature_set_comparison.ipynb)

A controlled comparison of my engineered feature set against the one supplied by the programme,
holding the model and the splits constant.

On a single split the supplied features win. Repeated across seven random splits, mine win six times
(mean ROC-AUC 0.682 against 0.674) using 12 fewer features. **The difference is not large enough to
act on**: the split-to-split standard deviation (~0.01) exceeds the gap between the two feature sets
(0.009). The honest conclusion is that they are comparable.

The notebook is included because the methodological point is the useful one — a single-split
comparison is not capable of separating these two feature sets, and it happened to select the one
split of seven where the ranking reverses.

## Limitations

- **The model is weak.** ROC-AUC of 0.665 indicates real but modest signal. It is not accurate enough
  for confident individual-level prediction, and the recommendation reflects that.
- **The value estimate is unstable.** Across seven splits the targeted programme returns between
  €17k and €92k, with a mean of €33k. The €92k figure comes from a single split and would overstate
  the opportunity threefold; the mean is quoted instead, with the range disclosed.
- **The threshold was tuned on the same split it is reported on.** The 0.30 cut-off is the
  profit-maximising value on the seed-42 test set, and the table above reports value on that same
  set, so the €4,208 is optimistic. The seven-split figures reuse 0.30 rather than re-tuning it, but
  0.30 was originally chosen on seed 42 (the split that returns the €92k outlier).
- **Two unvalidated assumptions.** The €112 margin figure is the median of `net_margin`, which the
  data dictionary does not confirm is annual. More importantly, the calculation assumes every
  discounted churner is retained — almost certainly optimistic, and the reason the recommendation is
  a pilot with a measured control group.
- **Feature importance shows association, not causation.** Margin and consumption rank highest, but
  the 35 price-derived columns collectively carry more importance than margin and consumption
  combined; their individual scores are diluted across correlated columns. The conclusion that price
  is not the primary driver rests mainly on the direct finding that churners and non-churners pay
  near-identical prices.
- **A field named `activity_new` (company activity category) appears in the data dictionary but not
  in the delivered file.** Company sector is plausibly predictive and its absence is a real gap.

## Possible extensions

- Cross-validation in place of a single split throughout.
- Hyperparameter tuning; only `n_estimators` was set.
- Handling class imbalance directly (`class_weight="balanced"` roughly triples recall at some cost to
  precision — tested in notebook 03 but not adopted, since threshold tuning is tied explicitly to the
  cost model).
- Measuring what fraction of discounted churners actually stay, which is the assumption the business
  case is most sensitive to.
